CASE STUDY 05 / LEARNING EXPERIENCE DESIGN + FRONT-END

CallFlow.
Clarity is a skill.

I kept coming back to one question: what if better documentation started during the conversation, not after it?

01 / THE WHY

I wanted to teach the habit,
not another system.

During my time in customer support, I noticed two quality issues that kept showing up: agents wouldn't always ask enough questions to fully understand the concern, and valuable details from phone calls weren't always making it into the notes.

To me, these weren't just documentation problems. They were clarity problems. It's easy to rush to a solution when you think you understand what someone needs. It's also easy to forget details once the call ends.

So I built CallFlow, a guided training experience for new agents to practise listening, asking the right questions, and turning a conversation into clear, useful documentation. It's not a CRM. It helps build the habits an agent can eventually take into any CRM.

“You don't have to know every answer. You need to know what to ask—and where to look.”

02 / THE EXPERIENCE

One conversation.
One clear step at a time.

Inspired by the simplicity of a Typeform-style flow, I kept each stage focused so new agents aren't trying to remember everything at once.

01GatherWho am I speaking with?
02UnderstandWhy are they calling?
03ParaphraseDid I get that right?
04Clarify + actWhat's missing? What next?
05DocumentWhat needs to be recorded?
01 / DESIGN INTENTION

Keep the person's name in sight.

Once the agent enters a caller's name, it stays at the top beside Call in progress. Not because it's technically necessary, but because it's a quiet reminder that there's a real person on the other side of the line.

I wanted to encourage agents to use the name naturally and make the conversation feel personal, without another pop-up telling them what to do.

02 / DESIGN INTENTION

Repeat it back to understand it.

Step 3 shows the reason for the call and asks the agent to paraphrase what they've heard, with room to add details they missed the first time.

Repeating and retrieving information are familiar parts of how we practise and remember things. I used that thinking to make the recap a small learning moment—not just another field to complete.

Learning inspiration: Gretchen Schmelzer, “Understanding Learning and Memory: The Neuroscience of Repetition” ↗

03 / THE HEART OF IT

Probing questions:
find the missing piece.

This was the part I really wanted to get right. An agent shouldn't need to memorize every City service to help a caller. They need support narrowing down the concern.

01 / RECOGNIZE

Start with how people actually speak.

Someone might say “my house taxes,” “the garbage truck skipped me,” or just “something's wrong with my bill.” The tool looks for everyday phrases, not only formal service names.

02 / CLARIFY

Ask two useful questions, not ten.

CallFlow checks what's already known and suggests up to two relevant questions. When a topic is vague, the first questions help identify the service before going any deeper.

03 / GUIDE

Help the agent know where to look.

The matched guide brings up links to official service information. If an answer reveals a more specific issue, the suggestions and reference links can change with it.

04 / REINFORCE

Make progress visible.

An answered question gets a clear completed state. It sounds small, but it's intentional: agents can see what they've confirmed and what still needs attention.

AN EXAMPLE / PROPERTY TAX

CALLER SAYS

“I need help with my house taxes.”

CALLFLOW MATCHES

Property Tax Billing

AGENT CLARIFIES

“Is it the amount, the due date or a payment?”

RESOURCE NARROWS

Property Tax Due Dates or Pre-Authorized Payments, depending on the answer

The point isn't to automate judgment. It's to help the agent build the confidence to ask the right next question.

04 / THE RESEARCH

Built from real demand.
Written for real language.

I didn't want to make up a list of what I thought 311 agents might get asked. I used the City's published data to decide what deserved a guide.

1.58M311 customer contacts in 2025

2Maximum suggested questions at a time

My research process

I started with the City of Toronto's 2025 311 Key Metrics Report ↗. I took the report's ten most common general inquiries and selected ten high-volume service-request categories from different divisions.

For example, property tax billing had 84,422 general inquiries, Vacant Home Tax had 63,850, and damaged residential bin lids had 24,768 service requests in the 2025 report. These are reported contact or request volumes—not performance results from CallFlow.

From there I mapped common wording and alternate phrases to each guide, drafted two useful question templates, and linked the matching public information pages. If someone says “house taxes” rather than “Property Tax Billing,” the library still has a way to help.

It's a local, rules-based library, not live AI. That choice keeps the training questions controlled and reviewable, without scraping City systems or sending callers' information to an external model.

2026 / IMPORTANT UPDATE

And I had to account for what changed.

Starting January 1, 2026, residential Blue Bin recycling collection and related service inquiries moved from the City to Circular Materials. That means a training tool built around 2025 request volume can't just repeat last year's routing. I added an explicit referral distinction so the guidance reflects the new responsibility.

05 / THE HANDOFF

Clear notes.
Without starting over.

At the end of the call, the information the agent entered becomes a structured note they can review, edit and copy into their existing CRM. No second system to maintain.

01

Only what was answered

Answered probing questions and responses make it into the notes; empty prompts don't.

02

Accountability at the end

Call timestamps, actions and mandatory resolution comments help the next person understand what happened.

03

Agent stays in control

The summary is editable before copying. The tool organizes what was captured; it doesn't invent an answer or a resolution.

06 / WHAT I LEARNED

The tool should eventually
teach itself out of a job.

The best part of this project wasn't adding more fields. It was deciding which ones were actually worth asking. I wanted the interface to feel like a quiet assistant: a visible name, a gentle progress cue, a little confirmation when something's done, and somewhere useful to look when the answer isn't obvious.

CallFlow is a working, self-initiated prototype built with fictional caller information. I haven't measured its effect on real agents yet. If I took it further, I'd test whether new agents capture more complete notes, ask more relevant questions and rely less on prompts over time.

Because the real goal isn't better form-filling. It's better habits that eventually feel second nature.
