01 / THE PROBLEM

A listing is the buyer’s first impression.

In a vehicle marketplace, the listing does the selling before a buyer ever calls. Better photos, a specific title, a useful description and realistic pricing all change how confident a buyer feels.

But dealers listing dozens of cars a week rarely get feedback beyond “views went down”. The typical result: one exterior photo, a vague title and a price that’s out of step with the market.

02 / DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Shape behaviour through clarity, not pressure.

The goal wasn’t another analytics dashboard. It was to influence what dealers do at the moment they’re editing, using four levers:

Clarity

Say exactly what to change, in plain language.

Timing

Give feedback while the listing is being written, not after it underperforms.

Feedback

Show the effect of each change straight away.

Trust

Explain the reasoning so the dealer believes the suggestion.

03 / FROM SCORE TO NEXT STEP

A task becomes a coaching moment.

A score on its own just tells a dealer they’re doing badly. ListIQ pairs it with the three changes that matter most for this specific listing:

  • Add interior photos: buyers want to see the dashboard, seats and condition.
  • Clarify the trim in the title so serious buyers find it.
  • Adjust the price closer to similar listings in the market.

Once applied, the dealer sees the listing move from 54 to 82 before publishing.

04 / WORKFLOW

AI that lives inside the editor.

If the assistant is a separate tool, dealers have to remember to use it. So I designed it into the existing listing flow:

  1. Create or edit a vehicle listing
  2. Receive a real-time quality score
  3. Review AI-generated recommendations
  4. Understand why each one matters
  5. Apply quick wins
  6. Preview the score and lead likelihood change
  7. Publish with more confidence
  8. Monitor performance after publishing

05 / DESIGNING FOR TRUST IN AI

Every suggestion has to explain itself.

The hardest part wasn’t the dashboard. It was designing a system that could earn a dealer’s trust. These decisions are built into the prototype:

  • Reasons, not orders. Each quick win carries its “why”: “Your price is 7% above similar listings,” not just “Lower price.”
  • Confidence is visible. Predictions like lead likelihood are labelled with a confidence level (e.g. “Medium confidence”) instead of being shown as certain.
  • Priority is explicit. High and Medium impact badges tell dealers where to spend limited time.
  • The method is one click away. A “How recommendations are generated” link sits right under the assistant’s header.
  • The dealer decides. Nothing changes until the dealer applies a fix. The AI suggests; the human publishes.

06 / VISUAL DIRECTION

Make AI feel practical, not intimidating.

Automotive dealer tools are usually dense and technical. ListIQ uses clear metric cards, soft layouts, strong contrast on the one action that matters, and coaching written in plain language.

07 / WHAT I’D DESIGN AND TEST NEXT

The questions an AI product has to answer.

  • When the AI is wrong: let dealers dismiss a suggestion with a reason, and use that feedback to improve future recommendations.
  • When data is thin: how confidence is calculated and shown for rare vehicles or new markets.
  • Gaming the score: make sure a high score reflects a genuinely better listing, not keyword stuffing.
  • Real dealers: watch three to five dealers list a car with and without the assistant, and measure time-to-publish and which suggestions they accept.

08 / REFLECTION

Designing AI for clarity, trust and action.

The assistant shouldn’t just do things for the user. It should help them understand what’s happening, why it matters and how to make a better decision. That shift, from automation to explanation, is what I’d carry into any AI product.
